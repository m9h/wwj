\section{Data Side: The Same Calibrated Lens Overturns Thompson's Discoverability Exponent}
\label{sec:dataside}

The weight-side thesis --- a heavy-tailed shape exponent is a calibrated \emph{diagnostic}, not a
\emph{lever}, and its point-estimate use is overturned by proper analysis --- has an exact data-side
twin. Thompson's \emph{Zeta Law of Discoverability} reads a single exponent $\beta$ off a frozen
representation's label-alignment spectrum and claims it forecasts \emph{data sufficiency}: $\beta>1$
means more data helps, $\beta<1$ means the representation is the bottleneck. We test that claim with the
same Bayesian machinery, and it does not survive.

\textbf{Covariance side: the diagnostic transfers cleanly.} Applying the \texttt{wwjd} validity-gated
posterior to the \emph{data} covariance spectrum (rather than a weight matrix) reproduces the weight-side
structure. Every learned foundation-model embedding is certifiably super-critical, $\alpha_{\rm cov}<2$
with posterior $P(\alpha<2)=1.00$ (functional and structural fMRI/sMRI models alike); every hand-built
classical feature set sits at or above the critical point, $\alpha_{\rm cov}\ge 2$. An exact
label-exchangeability permutation test on the learned-vs-classical split gives $p=\DsPermP$ (at the
attainable floor for the current modality count, and tightening as more models are added). This is a
genuine, target-free signature --- learned representations are heavy-tailed in their data covariance in a
way classical features are not --- and it is the clean part of the data-side story.

\textbf{Sufficiency side: the single exponent is falsified, and the model comparison names what it
misses.} We compare candidate predictors of data sufficiency (measured as the training-set size to reach
a fixed fraction of the achievable performance), on synthetic data with known ground truth and on real
neuroimaging (foundation-model embeddings predicting age, sex, and cross-model targets):

Three readings. (i) \textbf{Thompson's single $\beta$ does not forecast sufficiency} --- rank
correlation $\DsBetaSynth$ in synthetic, $\DsBetaReal$ on real data, and its regime call is inverted at
the easy end. The defect is structural: $\beta$ is the rank-decay of the alignment spectrum \emph{sorted
by magnitude}, and sorting discards \emph{which eigenmode} the signal occupies --- exactly the
eigenvalue position (high $\lambda$ = learnable with little data, low $\lambda$ = data-hungry) that sets
sufficiency. (ii) \textbf{The position-preserving statistics recover it}: keeping the same alignment
values in eigenvalue-rank order ($\rho_q$) restores a strong synthetic signal ($\DsRhoSynth$) and a real
one ($\DsRhoReal$); the full capacity+source theory (CBP) is exact against simulation (MAE $\DsCbpMae$).
(iii) \textbf{On real data even the correct theory loses to a flexible fit} --- CBP's parameter-free
prediction (skill $\DsCbpReal$) is beaten by a two-parameter power law (skill $\DsPlReal$) scored fairly
out-of-sample. The gap is not the physics but the estimator: per-mode target power carries a noise floor
$\sigma^2/M$ that is not reliably subtractable at cohort sizes on the order of $10^3$; a scaling analysis
shows a calibrated estimator recovers the true-spectrum curve once $M$ reaches $\sim10^4$ (an order of
magnitude beyond our labeled cohorts, within consortium reach).

\textbf{$\beta$ is Goodhart-able, like $\alpha$.} The discoverability exponent can be inflated by
\emph{compressing} a representation into fewer modes (a phenotype-histogram readout raises survival
$\beta$ from $0.4$ to $1.5$--$4.5$) without any gain in held-out separability (top-mode $\eta^2$ and
CV-AUC do not move). This is the data-side image of the weight-side $\alpha\!\to\!2$ regularizer:
a single shape exponent can be driven by discarding information rather than discovering it, so it must be
reported jointly with held-out quality, never alone.

\textbf{Why a language-model discoverability law may not transfer to neuroimaging.} Thompson's exponent
imports an intuition born in large language models --- that a clean spectral regularity of a frozen
representation forecasts what more data will buy. Two facts about neuroimaging foundation models make that
import fragile in ways that do not arise for language. First, \emph{these representations do not scale like
language models}: a systematic clinical-EEG benchmark (NeuroAtlas; \citealp{kontras2026neuroatlas}, 42
datasets, $\sim$260k hours) finds brain-age accuracy essentially uncorrelated with embedding dimension
(Spearman $\rho\!\approx\!-0.14$ to $-0.29$, n.s.) and EEG-specific pretraining no better than generic
time-series foundation models, or than a \emph{random-initialised} backbone, on most tasks. A
sufficiency law read off the representation spectrum has no language-model-style scaling regularity to
inherit here. Second, decoding on these models is partly \emph{confound-driven} (motor-imagery accuracy
collapses below chance once oculomotor artifacts are filtered), so an alignment spectrum computed on such
embeddings is in part measuring artifact rather than neural signal --- a concrete source of the gap between
our synthetic ($\rho_q\!=\!\DsRhoSynth$) and real ($\DsRhoReal$) recovery.

This directly qualifies our covariance-$\alpha$ signature, and a control pins down what it does and does not
mean. If super-criticality were merely geometric --- a consequence of projecting a heavy-tailed brain-data
covariance into high dimension --- then an \emph{untrained} random map of a real brain input should be
super-critical too. It is not: an untrained random Gaussian projection of structural morphometry stays at
the critical point ($\alpha_{\rm cov}\!\approx\!\DsAlphaRand$, tracking its input), pure noise is rejected by
the validity gate, and only the \emph{trained} foundation models are super-critical
($\alpha_{\rm cov}\!\approx\!\DsAlphaLearned$, $P(\alpha<2)=1.00$). So $\alpha_{\rm cov}<2$ is a real imprint
of pretraining, not a high-dimensional artifact. Yet NeuroAtlas shows those same pretrained models barely
outperform their random-initialised counterparts \emph{downstream}. The two facts together give the sharpest
statement of the paper's thesis: the exponent faithfully \emph{detects} that training reshaped the
representation's spectrum, while carrying essentially no information about whether that training improved
what can be predicted from it. A diagnostic of the representation, decoupled from its predictive utility.

The data side therefore closes the same loop as the weight side. A single calibrated shape exponent is a
useful \emph{diagnostic} of representational structure (the super-critical covariance signature), but as
a point-estimate \emph{predictor} of what data or training will buy you it is overturned --- structurally
for the sorted exponent, and Goodhart-able for the compressed one. The honest tools are the calibrated,
validity-gated diagnostic and a position-preserving (or robustly estimated) source statistic reported
with its uncertainty.
